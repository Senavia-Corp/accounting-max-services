What happens every month

Bookkeeping is not a January project. On a fixed monthly cycle:

  • Reconciliation. Every bank and credit card account is matched to the statement, so the balance in your books is the balance in the bank — not an estimate of it.
  • Categorization. Each transaction is posted to the account it belongs to. Anything ambiguous we ask you about that month, while you still remember what it was for.
  • Income and receivables. Revenue is recorded against what you actually invoiced, so work you have not been paid for shows up as unpaid instead of quietly disappearing.
  • Payroll, loan payments and owner draws. The three entries software guesses wrong most often, and the three that distort a whole year of profit when it does.
  • A closed month. You receive a profit and loss statement you can actually read, plus the short list of questions still open.

The IRS describes the same purposes: good records let you "monitor the progress of your business," "prepare accurate financial statements," and "support the income, expenses, and credits you report" (Publication 583, Rev. December 2024).

One business account. One personal account.

The most expensive habit we see is a single debit card paying for inventory and groceries. Untangling it later costs hours you pay for, and it weakens every number on the return. The IRS is blunt about the fix: "One of the first things you should do when you start a business is open a business checking account. You should keep your business account separate from your personal checking account." The reason is that your records have to "separate business from nonbusiness receipts and taxable from nontaxable income" (Publication 583, Rev. December 2024). Once the two are mixed, nobody can make that separation with certainty — only a guess in your favor, which is exactly what an examiner looks for.

What catching up in April really costs

Books rebuilt twelve months late have three problems. Deductions go missing, because nobody remembers what a charge from March was for. Decisions were made blind all year: you did not know your margin, so you priced on instinct. And the deadline lands with nothing ready — "Generally, a domestic partnership must file Form 1065 by the 15th day of the 3rd month following the date its tax year ended" (Instructions for Form 1065, 2025), and an S corporation return is due on the 15th day of the 3rd month after the end of its tax year (Instructions for Form 1120-S, 2025). For a calendar-year business that is March 15 — or the next business day when the 15th falls on a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday, which is why returns for the 2025 tax year were due March 16, 2026 (Instructions for Form 1065, 2025). Either way, a month before most owners start thinking about taxes at all.

Your books feed three separate filings

One ledger becomes your corporate tax return, the financial statements your bank or landlord asks for, and your Florida Sales Tax filing — due "on the 1st and late after the 20th day of the month following each reporting period," and where "a return must be filed for each reporting period, even if no tax is due" (Florida Department of Revenue, Florida Sales and Use Tax). When one set of books drives all three, the numbers agree with each other. When three people build them separately, they do not, and the mismatch is what gets noticed.

How long the records have to survive

Keeping the books is only half of it. The retention rules are the IRS's: "Keep records for 3 years" in the ordinary case, "6 years if you do not report income that you should report, and it is more than 25% of the gross income shown on your return," and "employment tax records for at least 4 years" (IRS, How long should I keep records?). If a return is ever questioned, "you must keep your business records available at all times for inspection by the IRS" (Publication 583, Rev. December 2024).
